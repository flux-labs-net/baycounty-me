Sandbags, shelters, evacuations and storm updates
Storm update

Hurricane Isaias Emergency Information

Bay County · Posted Oct 8, 2026 · Source

No sandbag sites, shelter openings or evacuation orders posted right now. Bay County and the cities announce sandbag sites when a storm threatens; they'll show here within minutes. Everything else is on Emergencies and alerts.

What to do

From Bay County, Ready.gov, the CDC, the Weather Service and FEMA

Before the season

  1. Know your zone and your plan

    Look up your evacuation zone, decide where you'd go (family or friends outside the zone first, a hotel, a shelter last) and how you'd get there. Plan for pets.

    Source: Bay County Disaster Preparedness Guide
  2. Build a kit for at least 3 days

    Water (a gallon per person per day), food that doesn't need cooking, medicines, a flashlight and batteries, a battery or hand-crank radio, phone chargers, cash, copies of IDs and insurance papers, and supplies for pets and babies.

    Source: Ready.gov
  3. Check your flood insurance now

    Homeowner and renter policies don't cover flooding. A new flood policy usually takes 30 days to start, so buying one when a storm is coming is too late.

    Source: Bay County Disaster Preparedness Guide; FloodSmart
  4. Register if you need help evacuating

    People who rely on oxygen or power-dependent equipment, or can't get around easily, should sign up for the Florida Special Needs Registry before a storm.

    Source: Florida Division of Emergency Management

Sandbags

  1. Where to get sand

    Bay County and the cities announce sand and sandbag sites when a storm threatens; they show up at the top of BayCounty.me's Hurricane prep page and on each government's news page. Bring a shovel; many sites are self-serve with a limit per household.

    Source: Bay County news
  2. Fill and place them right

    Fill bags about two-thirds full so they lie flat. Lay them like bricks, overlapping, with the folded end tucked under, and press each one down. Sandbags slow shallow water; they don't stop storm surge.

    Source: U.S. Army Corps of Engineers

Generator safety

  1. Never run it inside

    Run a generator outdoors only, at least 20 feet from doors, windows and vents. Never in a house, garage, carport, shed or crawl space, even with the door open. Carbon monoxide has no smell and kills in minutes.

    Source: CDC
  2. Put in carbon monoxide alarms

    Use battery or battery-backup CO alarms on every level of the home. If one sounds, or you feel dizzy, sick or weak, get to fresh air and call 911.

    Source: CDC
  3. Don't plug it into the house wiring

    Backfeeding through a dryer or wall outlet can kill line workers and neighbors. Use heavy-duty outdoor extension cords, or have an electrician install a transfer switch.

    Source: CDC
  4. Refuel it cool

    Turn it off and let it cool before adding fuel. Store gas in approved cans away from the house.

    Source: CDC

When a storm is coming

  1. Follow the orders for your zone

    When an evacuation order names your zone, leave early with a full tank. If you're outside the zones, shelter in place in an interior room. Mobile and manufactured homes are often included in orders anywhere in the county.

    Source: Bay County Emergency Services
  2. Secure the house

    Bring in or tie down anything that can blow away, cover windows, fill prescriptions, charge phones and power banks, and photograph your home and belongings for insurance.

    Source: Ready.gov

After the storm

  1. Stay out until it's safe

    Wait for officials to say an area is open. Stay away from downed lines and floodwater, which can hide debris and be electrified. Re-entry rules are on the county's re-entry page.

    Source: Bay County Emergency Services
  2. Watch for carbon monoxide and downed lines

    Generators, chainsaws, heat and downed power lines hurt and kill people in the days after a storm. Keep generators outside and at least 20 feet from the house, and treat every downed line as live.

    Source: National Weather Service
  3. Boil water until told otherwise

    If your water utility issues a boil-water notice, bring water to a rolling boil for one minute (or use bottled water) for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth and making ice until the notice is lifted.

    Source: City of Panama City Beach boil-water information
  4. Apply for help and report damage

    After a major disaster declaration with individual aid, apply at DisasterAssistance.gov or 800-621-3362. File insurance claims early and keep receipts for repairs and hotels.

    Source: FEMA

Written in plain English from the source on each step; follow the source for the full guidance. Checked Oct 27, 2026.

Rebuild money by neighborhood

By ZIP code, 2016 to 2026

After a declared disaster, FEMA can pay households to repair homes and rent somewhere while they rebuild. Flood insurance (the National Flood Insurance Program) pays policyholders after any flood. Both are published by FEMA as totals per ZIP code; no household is named.

FEMA housing aid is the Individuals and Households Program for major disasters that opened it (Hurricane Michael and Hurricane Sally since 2016): dollars approved for repairs, replacement and rent, for owners and renters added together. Flood insurance is building, contents and code-compliance payments on claims for properties in Bay County, by the year of the flood. Some ZIP codes cross the county line; only Bay County applicants and properties are counted. Money to governments (public assistance) isn't in these figures; for that, see where the Hurricane Michael money went.
